the story of a righteous man stripped of everything — his children, his wealth, his health — and of the long argument that follows over why. its author and date are unknown; it is set in the land of Uz, in the age of the patriarchs, and its hero is not even an Israelite. it is one of the old testament's wisdom books, and perhaps the boldest: it takes on the belief that suffering is always a punishment for sin, and refuses it.

a prose story frames a long poem. chapters 1–2 are the prologue: in a heavenly scene the accuser claims Job is faithful only because he is blessed, and God allows him to be tested. chapters 3–31 are the heart: Job curses the day he was born, and three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar — insist he must have done something wrong, while Job protests his innocence and demands an answer from God. chapters 32–37 bring in a younger voice, Elihu. chapters 38–41 are God's reply out of the storm: not an explanation, but a tour of the vastness of creation that leaves Job silent. in chapter 42 Job is restored — and God says that, unlike his friends, Job has spoken rightly of Him.

James holds up Job's endurance as an example, and Christians have long heard in his certainty that his Redeemer lives a longing that finds its answer in Jesus.
