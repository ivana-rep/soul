the second half of a single work with 1 Chronicles, traditionally attributed to Ezra and probably written in the Persian period, for the community that had returned from Babylon to a rebuilt but humble Jerusalem. having traced the story up to David, it now follows the temple and the kings of Judah, to explain both why the exile happened and why there was still hope after it.

it moves in two parts. chapters 1–9 are Solomon: the wisdom he asks for, and above all the temple — its building, its dedication, and God's answer to Solomon's prayer, the promise that if His people humble themselves, pray and turn back, He will hear and heal their land. chapters 10–36 follow the kings of Judah alone, from the split of the kingdom to the fall of Jerusalem in 586 bc. the pattern repeats: kings who seek the Lord, like Jehoshaphat, Hezekiah and Josiah, lead reforms; kings who abandon Him bring ruin. even Manasseh, the worst of them, is shown repenting in captivity and being restored — a story only Chronicles tells. the book ends with the temple burned and the people in exile, and then the decree of Cyrus, king of Persia, inviting them to go home and rebuild.

so the Hebrew bible closes with an invitation to go back up to Jerusalem. Jesus' words about the blood shed from Abel to Zechariah are often read as spanning the Hebrew scriptures, from their first book to this last one.
