the words of "the Teacher" (Qoheleth in Hebrew), who presents himself as a son of David and king in Jerusalem — traditionally Solomon, looking back near the end of his life; many scholars instead date the book centuries later, from its late Hebrew, and read the royal voice as a literary role. it belongs to the wisdom books, but asks the questions the others take for granted: what do we really gain from all our work, if everyone dies the same way?

it opens with its refrain, everything is meaningless — the Hebrew word means vapor, breath, something that vanishes as you grasp it — and nothing is new under the sun (chapter 1). chapters 1–2 are an experiment: wisdom, pleasure, great projects, wealth, all tried and found fleeting. chapter 3 sets life in its seasons, a time for everything. chapters 4–10 move through observations on injustice, work, money, power and the limits of wisdom. chapters 11–12 urge to enjoy life's gifts and to remember the Creator while young, and end with the conclusion: fear God and keep His commands.

honest about what doesn't add up, it makes room for doubt inside scripture. Paul echoes its key word when he writes that creation was subjected to futility, waiting to be set free.
