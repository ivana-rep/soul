the first book of the bible, traditionally attributed to Moses — a fugitive shepherd later called to confront Pharaoh and lead his people out of Egypt. its stories were handed down for generations before taking their final shape, which many scholars place around Israel's exile in Babylon: a people who had lost their land, their king and their temple, and needed to remember who they were, and whose they were.

it moves in two great parts. chapters 1–11 are about all of humanity: creation, man and woman made in God's image, the fall, Cain and Abel, the flood, Babel — a world made good, then fractured by sin, and a God who keeps refusing to let go of it. chapters 12–50 narrow to a single family: God calls Abraham and promises that through him all the families of the earth will be blessed, and that promise runs — fragile, often against the odds — through Isaac, Jacob and Joseph, all the way down to Egypt.

it is the book of beginnings: of the world, of sin, of grace, of the covenant. everything that follows, up to Jesus, is the story of that promise to Abraham being kept.
