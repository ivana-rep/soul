its author is unknown. early Christians proposed Paul, Barnabas, Apollos and others; the church father Origen said that only God knows who wrote it. it was addressed to believers — very likely Jewish Christians — who, worn down by pressure and persecution, were tempted to drift away from faith in Christ. more a sermon than a letter, its author calls it "a word of exhortation"; many scholars date it before the destruction of the temple in ad 70, though this is debated.

chapters 1–10 build a single argument: Jesus is greater than the angels, greater than Moses, greater than the priests of the old covenant — a high priest like Melchizedek, who offered Himself once for all, inaugurating the new covenant promised by Jeremiah. along the way come sharp warnings against falling away. from chapter 10 the tone turns to exhortation: hold on to hope; chapter 11 recalls the great witnesses of faith, from Abel to the prophets; chapter 12 calls the reader to run the race with eyes fixed on Jesus; chapter 13 closes with practical instructions.

it is the new testament's great bridge to the old: it reads the whole sacrificial system of Israel as a shadow, and Christ as the reality it pointed to.
