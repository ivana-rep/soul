[Mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_the_Evangelist) is traditionally identified with the John Mark of the book of Acts, a cousin of Barnabas, whose mother's house in Jerusalem was a meeting place for the first christians (Acts 12:12). He set out with Paul and Barnabas on their first missionary journey but turned back halfway (Acts 13:13), and when Paul refused to take Him a second time, Paul and Barnabas parted ways over Him (Acts 15:37-39). years later, from prison, Paul asked for Him again: "bring Mark with you when you come, for He will be helpful to me" (2 Timothy 4:11). [Papias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papias_of_Hierapolis), a bishop of the early second century, says Mark was Peter's interpreter and wrote down what Peter taught about Jesus. tradition makes Him the founder of the church of Alexandria; in the ninth century two Venetian merchants took His body to Venice, where [Saint Mark's basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mark%27s_Basilica) was built to hold it. His feast is April 25, and His symbol among the [four evangelists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Evangelists) is the winged lion.

---

the shortest of the four gospels, and most likely the earliest, traditionally attributed to Mark, who was not one of the twelve apostles. many scholars date it around the years 65–70, possibly written in Rome, for christians facing persecution and, soon after, the destruction of the temple in Jerusalem.

it moves fast — "immediately" is one of its favourite words — and in three stages. chapters 1–8 are Jesus' ministry in Galilee: healings, exorcisms, parables, crowds who are amazed but don't really understand who He is. at the center, in chapter 8, Peter finally says it: "You are the Messiah". chapters 8–10 are the road to Jerusalem, where Jesus three times announces that He must suffer, die and rise, and three times His disciples miss the point. chapters 11–16 are the last week: the entry into Jerusalem, the cross, the empty tomb.

it is the gospel of a hidden Messiah, recognized not through His miracles but at the foot of the cross, where it is a Roman centurion who says that this man was truly the Son of God. most scholars think Matthew and Luke both built on it.
