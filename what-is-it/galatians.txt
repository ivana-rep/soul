a letter from the apostle Paul to churches in the region of Galatia, in what is now central Turkey, which he had founded himself. after he left, other teachers arrived insisting that gentile believers had to be circumcised and follow the law of Moses to belong fully to God's people. Paul's reply is urgent and angry — it is the only one of his letters with no thanksgiving at the start. it was written somewhere between the late 40s and the mid-50s; the exact date, and which Galatian churches it addresses, are debated.

it moves in three steps. chapters 1–2 are Paul's own story: a gospel received not from men but from Jesus Christ, his years as a persecutor, his agreement with the leaders in Jerusalem, and the day he confronted Peter in Antioch for drawing back from eating with gentiles. chapters 3–4 are the argument: Abraham was counted righteous because he believed, before the law existed; the law was a guardian until Christ came; those who believe are sons and heirs, not slaves. chapters 5–6 are what freedom looks like — not an excuse for the flesh, but life led by the Spirit, whose fruit is love, joy, peace.

it is the new testament's charter of christian freedom, and the letter on which Luther wrote one of his best-known commentaries.
