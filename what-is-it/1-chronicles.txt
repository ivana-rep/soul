the first half of a single work with 2 Chronicles, traditionally attributed to Ezra — a priest and scribe who helped lead the Jewish return from exile in Babylon. most scholars place it in the Persian period, after the return, when a small community rebuilding Jerusalem around a modest temple needed to know whether it was still the people of the promise. Chronicles answers by retelling Israel's history from the beginning, with the temple and David's line at the center.

it moves in two parts. chapters 1–9 are genealogies, from Adam to the families who resettled Jerusalem after the exile — a long list that is really a claim: those who came back belong to the whole story. chapters 10–29 are David: after Saul's death, David takes Jerusalem, brings the ark there with music and joy, receives God's promise of a house that will last forever, and spends his last years preparing for the temple his son Solomon will build, organizing priests, Levites and singers. compared with Samuel, it leaves out much of David's failure, including Bathsheba: less a biography than a portrait of the king as a worshiper.

in the Hebrew bible, Chronicles is the very last book. Matthew opens the new testament the same way it begins — with a genealogy running through David — to show that the promise has reached Jesus.
