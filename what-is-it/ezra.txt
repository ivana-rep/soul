a historical book that, in the Hebrew bible, forms a single work with Nehemiah. it takes its name from Ezra — a priest and scribe learned in the law of Moses — and covers roughly a century after the exile: in 539 bc Cyrus of Persia conquered Babylon and soon allowed the deported Jews to return home. Ezra is about what a broken people does with a second chance.

it moves in two parts. chapters 1–6 are the first return, under Cyrus's decree: led by Zerubbabel and the priest Jeshua, the returnees rebuild the altar and lay the foundations of the temple — the old men who remembered Solomon's temple weeping, while the young shouted for joy — then face years of opposition until, spurred on by the prophets Haggai and Zechariah, they finish the temple around 515 bc. chapters 7–10 jump about sixty years ahead: Ezra arrives from Babylon with a second group and with the law, after fasting and praying for a safe journey rather than asking the king for an escort; the book ends in a painful crisis over marriages with the surrounding peoples, resolved with hard separations.

the temple rebuilt here, later enlarged by Herod, is the one where Jesus was presented as a child, where He taught, and where He drove out the merchants.
