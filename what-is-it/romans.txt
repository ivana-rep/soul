a letter from the apostle Paul — a former persecutor of the church turned its most influential missionary — written around the year 57 from Corinth, to a church in Rome he had never visited. the community was mixed: Jewish believers, some returning after the emperor Claudius had expelled the Jews from Rome, and gentile believers who had grown in number in the meantime. many scholars read tension between the two groups as the background to much of what Paul writes.

so the letter sets out the gospel from the ground up: everyone, Jew and gentile alike, falls short (chapters 1–3); everyone is made right with God the same way, by faith, as a gift (chapters 3–5); that new standing changes how we live, through the Spirit, with nothing able to separate us from God's love (chapters 6–8); Israel still has a place in God's plan (chapters 9–11); and faith takes shape in everyday life — in the community, toward outsiders, between the strong and the weak (chapters 12–16).

the most systematic explanation of the gospel in the new testament, and the book behind some of the church's great turning points: Augustine's conversion, Luther's reformation, Wesley's heart "strangely warmed".
