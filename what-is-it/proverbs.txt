a collection of short, memorable sayings on how to live well, mostly attributed to Solomon — David's son and Israel's king, renowned for his wisdom. it grew over time: some sections are credited to Solomon, others to "the wise", one to the men of king Hezekiah who copied out more of his proverbs, and the last ones to two otherwise unknown figures, Agur and Lemuel. its aim is practical, not abstract: how to speak, work, handle money, choose friends, live in a family.

chapters 1–9 are longer poems, a parent teaching a child, with wisdom pictured as a woman calling out in the streets, set against folly. chapters 10–22 are the classic one-line proverbs of Solomon. chapters 22–24 gather "sayings of the wise", a section that closely resembles an older Egyptian teaching. chapters 25–29 are more proverbs of Solomon, collected under Hezekiah. chapters 30–31 close with Agur, Lemuel, and an acrostic poem on the capable wife.

its foundation is stated at the start: the fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge. in chapter 8 wisdom speaks as present beside God at creation, an image the new testament takes up when it calls Christ the wisdom of God.
