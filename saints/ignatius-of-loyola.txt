↳ bio
feast day: July 31
1491 | born at the castle of Loyola, above Azpeitia (Gipuzkoa, Basque Country), as Íñigo López de Oñaz y Loyola, youngest of thirteen children; His mother dies soon after and a blacksmith's wife, María de Garín, nurses Him (1491 is the accepted year; He gave His own age inconsistently, so older sources say 1493 or 1495)
c. 1506 | sent as a page to the household of Juan Velázquez de Cuéllar, chief treasurer of Castile, at Arévalo: court life, gambling, duels, women, and a taste for chivalric romances
1515 | prosecuted at Azpeitia, together with His brother Pero, for night-time brawling during carnival
1517 | after Velázquez's death enters the service of the Duke of Nájera, viceroy of Navarre
1521 | on May 20, at the siege of Pamplona, persuades the garrison to resist the French; a cannonball shatters His right leg. carried back to Loyola, He has the badly set bone broken again and a protruding stump sawn off, without anaesthetic, so He can still wear fashionable boots
1521 | convalescing at Loyola, finds no romances in the house and reads instead Ludolph of Saxony's Life of Christ and Jacopo da Varazze's Golden Legend. He catches Himself asking "what if I did what [Francis of Assisi](post.html?p=saints/francis-of-assisi.txt) did, or Dominic?", and notices something new: daydreams of knightly glory delight Him while they last and leave Him "dry and dissatisfied", while dreams of imitating the saints leave Him "satisfied and joyful" even afterwards. this is the seed of the discernment of spirits
1521 | copies the words of Christ in red ink and those of Mary in blue, about three hundred pages
1522 | in March leaves Loyola for Montserrat: makes a written general confession lasting three days, gives His fine clothes to a beggar, hangs His sword and dagger at the altar of Our Lady and keeps an all-night vigil of arms on the eve of the Annunciation (March 24-25)
1522 | about eleven months at Manresa: begging, long hours of prayer, severe penances. months of scruples drive Him to the edge of suicide; He fasts a whole week until His confessor stops Him, then decides never to confess past sins again and is freed
1522 | beside the river Cardoner "the eyes of His understanding began to be opened": an illumination He later says taught Him more than everything else in His life put together. at Manresa He starts writing the notes that become the Spiritual Exercises, testing on Himself first what He would later give to others
1523 | pilgrimage to Jerusalem (arrives September 4); wants to stay for good, but the Franciscan custodian orders Him to leave
1524 | back in Barcelona, aged about thirty-three, sits among schoolboys to learn Latin grammar, supported by Isabel Roser
1526 | studies at Alcalá; gives the Exercises to ordinary people and is investigated by the Inquisition
1527 | jailed at Alcalá for forty-two days, then at Salamanca for twenty-two days, chained by the foot; the Dominicans examine His notes and find no heresy, but forbid Him to tell mortal from venial sin in His teaching until He has studied four more years
1528 | in February reaches Paris "alone and on foot"; studies at the Collège de Montaigu, then Sainte-Barbe, where He shares a room with Peter Faber and Francis Xavier
1533 | licentiate in philosophy; around this time He starts signing as Ignatius, probably out of devotion to Ignatius of Antioch
1534 | on August 15, at Montmartre, He and six companions (Faber, Xavier, Laínez, Salmerón, Rodrigues, Bobadilla) vow poverty, chastity and a pilgrimage to Jerusalem, or, if that proves impossible, to put themselves at the pope's disposal
1535 | master of arts at the University of Paris ("Master Ignatius")
1537 | ordained priest in Venice on June 24; war with the Turks blocks the voyage to Jerusalem
1537 | in November, at the chapel of La Storta outside Rome, has a vision in which "God the Father placed him with Christ His Son"; tradition adds the words "I will be favourable to you in Rome"
1538 | says His first Mass on Christmas night at Santa Maria Maggiore, in the chapel of the crib, after waiting more than a year
1539 | the companions decide to become a religious order; Paul III approves their "formula" orally at Tivoli on September 3
1540 | on September 27 the bull Regimini militantis Ecclesiae founds the Society of Jesus
1541 | elected first superior general; He refuses the first vote and accepts the second only on His confessor's advice
1544 | from February 1544 to February 1545 keeps the surviving part of His Spiritual Diary, a day-by-day discernment, at Mass, on whether Jesuit churches should have fixed income, full of tears and consolation
1546 | admits Isabel Roser and two companions to vows under His obedience, then has them released; in 1547 Paul III agrees the Society will have no women's branch
1547 | with Juan de Polanco begins writing the Constitutions of the Society, which prescribe the theology of [Thomas Aquinas](post.html?p=saints/thomas-aquinas.txt) in Jesuit schools; He keeps revising them until His death
1548 | on July 31 Paul III approves the Spiritual Exercises with the brief Pastoralis officii; the first printed edition (in Latin) comes out in Rome the same year. the Jesuit school at Messina opens, and education becomes the Society's main work
1551 | opens the Roman College, the future Pontifical Gregorian University, followed by the German College in 1552
1553 | on March 26 writes the letter on obedience to the Jesuits of Portugal
1553 | from 1553 to 1555 dictates His memoirs to Luís Gonçalves da Câmara, speaking of Himself in the third person as "the pilgrim"
1556 | dies in Rome on July 31, probably of malaria, without the last rites, the end coming sooner than expected; the Society then has about a thousand members in a hundred houses. His relics now rest in the church of the Gesù, under Andrea Pozzo's altar
1609 | beatified by Paul V
1622 | canonized on March 12 by Gregory XV, the same day as Francis Xavier, Teresa of Ávila, Philip Neri and Isidore the Farmer
1922 | Pius XI declares Him heavenly patron of spiritual exercises and retreats
2013 | Jorge Mario Bergoglio becomes Pope Francis, the first Jesuit pope; that December he canonizes Peter Faber, and in 2022-23 devotes a cycle of fourteen catecheses to discernment
2025 | the Society of Jesus counts 13,768 members worldwide

↳ quotes
\ man is created to praise, reverence, and serve God our Lord, and by this means to save his soul. the other things on the face of the earth are created for man to help him in attaining the end for which he is created.
~ — Spiritual Exercises 23, principle and foundation

\ we should not prefer health to sickness, riches to poverty, honor to dishonor, a long life to a short life. our one desire and choice should be what is more conducive to the end for which we are created.
~ — Spiritual Exercises 23

\ it is not much knowledge that fills and satisfies the soul, but the intimate understanding and relish of the truth.
~ — Spiritual Exercises 2

\ the first point is to give thanks to God our Lord for the favors received. the second point is to ask for grace to know my sins and to rid myself of them. the third point is to demand an account of my soul from the time of rising up to the present examination. the fourth point will be to ask pardon of God our Lord for my faults. the fifth point will be to resolve to amend with the grace of God.
~ — Spiritual Exercises 43, method of the general examen

\ in time of desolation we should never make any change, but remain firm and constant in the resolution and decision which guided us the day before the desolation.
~ — Spiritual Exercises 318, rules for the discernment of spirits

\ let him consider, too, that consolation will soon return.
~ — Spiritual Exercises 321

\ love ought to manifest itself in deeds rather than in words.
~ — Spiritual Exercises 230, contemplation to attain love

\ take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding, and my entire will, all that I have and possess. Thou hast given all to me. to Thee, O Lord, I return it. all is Thine, dispose of it wholly according to Thy will. give me Thy love and Thy grace, for this is sufficient for me.
~ — Spiritual Exercises 234, the Suscipe

\ when he was thinking of those things of the world, he took much delight in them, but afterwards, when he was tired and put them aside, he found himself dry and dissatisfied. but when he thought of going to Jerusalem barefoot, not only was he consoled when he had these thoughts but even after putting them aside he remained satisfied and joyful.
~ — Autobiography 8, dictated in the third person

\ help me, Lord, for I find no remedy in men nor in any creature. Yourself, Lord, show me where I may find it; even though I should have to chase after a puppy that it may give me the remedy, I will do it.
~ — Autobiography 23, at Manresa, in the middle of the scruples

\ every time, at any hour, he wished to find God, he found Him.
~ — Autobiography 99, da Câmara recording Ignatius in old age

\ pray as if everything depended on God, work as if everything depended on you.
~ — widely attributed to Ignatius, but not His: it comes from Gábor Hevenesi's Scintillae Ignatianae (1705), and the popular version reverses the original, which says to trust God as if everything depended on you, and to work as if God alone would do everything

\ go forth and set the world on fire.
~ — said by tradition to be His farewell to Francis Xavier in 1540; not found in any of His writings or letters

↳ what resonates with me
* Ignatius is the saint of method: [Francis of Assisi](post.html?p=saints/francis-of-assisi.txt) shows what holiness looks like, Ignatius hands you the exercises to get there, and they still work in a life full of meetings and deadlines.
* the whole of Ignatian discernment starts on a sickbed, with a bored soldier noticing the aftertaste of his own daydreams. fantasies of glory delighted Him while they lasted and left Him dry; fantasies of following Christ left Him at peace long after. He didn't judge His thoughts by how they felt in the moment, but by what they left behind. the same test works on a phone at eleven at night: the scroll is pleasant while it lasts, and the emptiness afterwards is the information. !!the truth of a desire shows in its aftertaste!!. Paul says the same thing in [Galatians 5](post.html?p=bible/galatians_5-1-26.txt): you know the spirit by its fruit, by what it leaves growing in you (love, joy, peace), not by the intensity of the impulse.
* the examen in Exercises 43 has five points, and the first one is not "find your sins" but "give thanks". the order matters: you look back on the day first as a gift, and only then as a record. in 1972 George Aschenbrenner called it an "examen of consciousness" rather than of conscience, because the question is less "what did i do wrong?" than "where was God today, and did I notice?". it's not a productivity review or a journal of self-improvement: it's a day reread with someone else in the room. [Psalms 139:23-24](post.html?p=bible/psalms_139-23-24.txt) * is the right posture: "search me, O God", not "let me audit myself", and [1 Thessalonians 5:18](post.html?p=bible/1-thessalonians_5-16-18.txt) is its first point in one line ("be thankful in all circumstances"). Ignatius practiced it Himself first: He told da Câmara that He put the examen with its chart of lines into the Exercises because it had been useful to Him. the famous line about Him ordering Jesuits never to skip the examen even if they skip every other prayer is a modern myth (Barton Geger SJ traced it in 2017), but the habit behind it is real.
* rule 318 is perhaps the most practical sentence in the whole book: in desolation, never change a decision you made in consolation. no resignation letters at two in the morning, no breaking up after a bad week, no abandoning a vocation because prayer has gone dry. desolation is real but temporary ("consolation will soon return", rule 321), and it is the worst possible adviser. Ignatius adds that in desolation you shouldn't just wait it out: you stay in prayer a little longer than planned, you act against the pull (the later Latin tag is agere contra). it's the opposite of following your feelings, and also the opposite of ignoring them: you read them, and refuse to let them hold the pen.
* the Principle and Foundation (Exercises 23) asks for "indifference": not preferring health to sickness, riches to poverty, a long life to a short one. it sounds cold until you see what it means: being free enough from every outcome that you can actually choose what is better. most of our decisions are made by the things we're afraid to lose. [Isaiah 43:7](post.html?p=bible/isaiah_43-7.txt) * ("made for My glory") is the same starting point, and it's where the Jesuit motto ad maiorem Dei gloriam comes from: not "the glory of God" but "the greater glory", the more, the magis, the choice that serves the end a little better than the alternative.
* Exercises 1 compares the spiritual exercises to walking and running: you train the soul the way you train the body, with repetitions, timetables, someone who knows the route. [1 Corinthians 9:24-27](post.html?p=bible/1-corinthians_9-24-27.txt) * says the same thing about the athlete. the full form is a thirty-day silent retreat in four "weeks" (sin and mercy, the life of Christ, the passion, the resurrection), but annotation 19 already allows a version for someone "engaged in public affairs or necessary business", an hour and a half a day for months. in 1520s Spain this was radical: a layman giving lay people a way to pray without a monastery, and the director told (annotation 15) to step aside and "permit the Creator to deal directly with the creature". that freedom is exactly why the Inquisition jailed Him twice.
* the Exercises ask you to pray with your imagination: compose the place, put yourself in the Gospel scene, look at the people, hear what they say, even make yourself "a poor little unworthy slave" at the manger. it's prayer for people who think too much. "it is not much knowledge that fills and satisfies the soul, but the intimate understanding and relish of the truth": you can read every commentary on a passage and never taste it. [Augustine](post.html?p=saints/augustine-of-hippo.txt) told his readers to go inward to find the truth; Ignatius gives them a concrete way in.
* the man who wrote the rules for scruples (Exercises 345-351) had nearly killed Himself over them. at Manresa He confessed the same sins again and again, never sure He had said everything, until He was ready to "chase after a puppy" if it would cure Him. out of that came one of the sharpest lines in the book: the enemy makes a delicate conscience more delicate and a lax one more lax, pushing each toward its own extreme. for anyone with an anxious, perfectionist conscience, it's a relief to be guided by someone who has been all the way down that road and found the way out.
* the Suscipe ("take, Lord, and receive all my liberty, my memory, my understanding, and my entire will") closes the Exercises, inside the contemplation to attain love, which opens with two plain observations: love shows itself in deeds more than in words, and love is a sharing of goods between two parties. the prayer is not a renunciation but the lover's answer: You gave me everything, i give it back, Your love is enough. it's the same movement as [i belong entirely to You](post.html?p=prayers/i-belong-entirely-to-you.txt) and [not my will, but Yours](post.html?p=prayers/not-my-will-but-yours.txt) *.
* the most quoted line attributed to Him, "pray as if everything depended on God, work as if everything depended on you", isn't His, and the original is stranger and better: Hevenesi wrote that you should trust God as if everything depended on you, and work as if God alone would do everything. the paradox refuses to split the job between God and you; both are fully at work at once, which is the point of [salvation is synergy](post.html?p=commonplace/salvation-is-synergy.txt).
* Jerónimo Nadal, one of His closest companions, described Ignatius as "contemplative even in action", and da Câmara recorded that in old age He could find God "at any hour" He wished. He spent His last fifteen years at a desk in Rome, governing an order by letter (thousands of them survive). the point of the Exercises was never to stay on retreat: it was to come back to the office, the family, the inbox, and find God there. that's what "finding God in all things" means for someone who will never live in a monastery.
* i'm not very familiar with His life and experiences yet: i became curious about Him listening to the spiritual life podcast, when [Brené Brown](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jddNUhUL-uI&t=1217s) and [Andrew Garfield](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9yxeax_IdKY&t=1741s) talk about how they pray the examen.
* He left behind the Society of Jesus (about a thousand members when He died, 13,768 today), a global network of schools that began with Messina and the Roman College, now the Gregorian University, and a book the Church approved in 1548 that is still the standard method of retreat almost five centuries later: Pius XI made Him patron of spiritual exercises in 1922. canonized the same day as Teresa of Ávila and His old roommate Francis Xavier, He also left the Church its first Jesuit pope, Francis, who taught discernment to the whole Church in fourteen catecheses. and every evening, in houses that have never heard of Manresa, someone sits down for ten minutes and asks where God was today.
