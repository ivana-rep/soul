↳ bio
feast day: August 11
1194 | born in Assisi as Chiara Offreduccio, eldest daughter of a noble family
1212 | at eighteen, hears Francis preach and leaves Her father's house on Palm Sunday to join Him at the Porziuncula; He cuts Her hair and clothes Her in a plain habit
1212 | settles at San Damiano, founding what becomes the Order of Poor Ladies, later the Poor Clares; Her mother and sister soon join Her
c. 1216 | becomes abbess of San Damiano, a role She holds for nearly forty years
1228 | Pope Gregory IX grants Her community the Privilege of Poverty, the right to own nothing at all — an earlier 1216 version attributed to Innocent III is disputed by scholars
1240 | though gravely ill, has Herself carried to the wall of San Damiano with the Blessed Sacrament as Frederick II's Saracen mercenaries besiege Assisi; the attackers retreat
1253 | writes Her Rule, the first monastic rule known to have been composed by a woman
1253 | Pope Innocent IV approves Her Rule and confirms the Privilege of Poverty, two days before Her death
1253 | dies at San Damiano on August 11, after nearly three decades of illness that had left Her largely bedridden
1255 | canonized by Pope Alexander IV, just over two years after Her death
1263 | Pope Urban IV renames Her order the Order of Saint Clare
1958 | Pope Pius XII declares Her patron saint of television, after an account that She once saw and heard a Christmas Mass on the wall of Her room while too ill to attend in person

↳ what resonates with me
* as Saint [Francis of Assisi](post.html?p=saints/francis-of-assisi.txt), Clare came from the same soil in Assisi and gave up the same things, but She had to fight for the right to keep them — a fight Francis, as a man and the order's own founder, never had to wage in quite the same way.
* for over two decades Clare fought successive popes for the right to own absolutely nothing — the Privilege of Poverty — while even Francis's own male order, after His death, gradually accepted property and income under the same institutional pressure She refused. !!no to security, on principle, for thirty years!!, and She won, two days before She died. it's easy to admire poverty as an idea; holding the line on it against people with actual power to make you stop is a different kind of endurance.
* in 1240, with Frederick II's mercenaries at the gates and Clare too ill to stand on Her own, She had Herself carried to the monastery wall holding the Blessed Sacrament instead of any weapon — and the attackers withdrew. it's a strange kind of courage: Her only action, in the moment that mattered most, was to remain present and hold still. sometimes protecting what you've built doesn't look like resistance, it looks like refusing to move.
* Her closest friendship may have been one She carried on entirely through letters: Agnes of Prague, a Bohemian princess who turned down marriage to Frederick II himself — the same emperor whose mercenaries would later besiege Assisi — to follow the same path Clare had chosen, and whom Clare mentored for over twenty years without, as far as the record shows, ever meeting her in person. Clare's advice to her keeps returning to one image: gaze upon that mirror each day, She writes, not a mirror that shows your face but one that shows Christ — look, consider, contemplate, imitate. centuries before every surface became a screen showing you back to yourself, Clare was already making the case for !!the one reflection actually worth checking yourself against!!.
* i've been to Her church in Assisi twice, and visited San Damiano, where She actually lived — there's an incredible peace there, hard to describe. Her determination in holding to what She believed, against real pressure, for that long, is honestly astonishing to me.
* Her broader legacy: canonized just over two years after Her death — remarkably fast for the period — Her Rule stands as the first monastic rule known to have been written by a woman, and in 1958 She was named patron saint of television after an account that She once saw and heard Mass on the wall of Her room while too ill to attend in person: the woman who fought hardest to own nothing became, seven centuries later, !!patron of the screens no one can put down!!.
